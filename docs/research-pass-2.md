# Research Pass #2 — Deep Knowledge Base for Aimee's Three-Layer, Mystcraft-Style Comfy Game (v2, corrected)

> **v2 note:** This revision removes cozy-genre framing that had crept in as prescription. "Comfy" here means *session ergonomics* — turn-based, interruptible, sleep-friendly, nothing taken from you while the app is closed — not the cozy genre's low-stakes contract. High tension, greed, scarcity, and catastrophic in-session loss are all in-bounds; the open question is which *layers* carry them.

## Summary

This pass digs into the mechanics behind your design pillars: Mystcraft's symbol/instability grammar, FF12-style condition→action automation, world upkeep/decay systems, nested meta-progression, and player-authored procedural generation. The single most important finding: **Mystcraft already implemented, and then repeatedly rebalanced, almost exactly the "valuable worlds crumble unless sustained" mechanic you want** — and the way it did it (hidden instability scores, block-profiling, deck-based severity draws) plus how its successor RFTools converted that into a clean *power-upkeep-per-tick* model gives you two contrasting templates for your anchoring cost. Equally important: the "programming your party is the joy" instinct is validated across FF12, Dragon Age, Gladiabots, Autonauts and Unicorn Overlord, but every one of them shows the same failure mode — automation that trivializes the content it automates. Below, each topic covers how the mechanic works, what players loved, what failed, and design levers, then closes with the sharpest open questions and the sourcing block.

Everything is framed as raw material, not a recommendation to clone anything.

<summary>1. MYSTCRAFT — symbol/page system, instability, travel, and successors</summary>

### How the symbol/page system works
Mystcraft is a Minecraft mod (by XCompWiz) that recreates Myst's Age-writing. You author "Ages" (instanced dimensions) by binding symbol **pages** into a **Descriptive Book**, then link into the generated world; a **Linking Book** written on-site is your one-way ticket home.

- **Page acquisition** (deliberately gated so symbols are earned, not given):
  - **Archivist villagers** trade symbol pages for emeralds; rare/valuable symbols (e.g., Diamond Ore) cost up to 64 emeralds. The villager cycles a different page each restock.
  - **Abandoned Library / temples** spawn near spawn in every Age with lecterns holding up to five pages, plus a hidden chest with up to eight more and sometimes a Notebook.
  - **Village Archivist's House** holds up to two pages, a Writing Desk, bookshelves.
  - **Random dungeon/mineshaft loot** chests.
  - **Copying**: pages are duplicated using ink at a Writing Desk into Notebooks — so once you own a symbol, you can reuse it across many books.
- **Symbol categories** (grammar slots): Biome controllers (exactly one required; e.g. Single Biome, Tiled, Tiny), Biome modifiers (one per vanilla/modded biome), Terrain controllers (standard, cave world, floating islands, void, skylands), Celestial bodies (Sun/Moon/Stars — at least one Sun required, with Length/position modifiers), Lighting, Weather, Sky/Fog/Cloud **color** modifiers (mixable, gradients over time), Materials/block modifiers (ores, tendrils, glowstone), Features (villages, strongholds, mineshafts, dungeons, obelisks), and Effects (Charged, Meteors, Explosions, Accelerated, Scorched — these duplicate instability effects but can *reduce* net instability).
- **Grammar & random fill**: An Age is generated from an ordered list of symbols read like a sentence. **Incomplete descriptions are filled randomly** — if you don't specify enough biomes for your controller, the game picks extras; unused/dangling modifiers get auto-assigned or cleared by a **Clear Modifiers** page (which itself adds a little instability per dangling modifier). Duplicate critical symbols (two terrain controllers) add a large instability spike and the second is ignored.
- **Version change worth noting**: Before v0.10, you could spam-generate random Descriptive Books, read which symbols they used, copy those, and sidestep instability entirely. v0.10+ blanked the pages in random books, forcing genuine symbol collection — a deliberate move to protect the collect→author loop.

### The instability system (this is your "crumbling worlds" precedent)
Instability = how dangerous/destructive an Age is versus the overworld. It is **not** set once at worldgen; it is recalculated during play (roughly every 100 chunks explored) based on the actual blocks present.

- **What triggers it**: (a) **Valuable/rare materials** — a "Dense Ores" world is *always* unstable; the mod runs a background **profiling pass** that measures how rare each block is, and "if an Age contains a significant amount of rare materials, instability will be added to compensate." Diamond seas / gold land literally destabilize. (b) **Conflicting/greedy symbols** — Single Biome + multiple biomes, "Accelerated" (faster farms/growth), "Bright Lighting" (easier mining), duplicate controllers, dangling modifiers. (c) Missing required symbols.
- **Five internal severity categories, drawn via "card deck mechanics"** (the wiki's own words), so effects escalate probabilistically:
  1. **Surface-only status effects** (nausea, slowness, mining fatigue, hunger, blindness, poison, weakness, wither — mobs get regen/resistance) when exposed to sky.
  2. **Global status effects** (same, but underground too, stacking to stronger potion levels).
  3. **Harmful environment**: random explosions, scorched surface (fire ~every 5s, 4–5 dmg to mobs), charged (lightning).
  4–5. **World-destroying**: **meteors** (destroy everything given time, drop ores), **decay blocks** (black = collapses columns through bedrock; blue = spreads by mining-hardness; red = by blast-resistance; purple = spreads through liquids, 85% chance in Dense Ores worlds; white = fastest, damages moving players, eats other decay), and **crumble/erosion** (diamond→coal, stone→cobble→gravel→sand, eventually a void world).
- **Balancing value vs. stability**: Players deliberately **accept a known bad effect to suppress worse ones** — adding **Charged** or **Scorched Earth/Explosions** symbols adds *stability* points, letting a Dense Ores world survive long enough to strip-mine. The community meta was: build a quarry/frame-bore, chunk-load it, let the Age self-destruct while you harvest remotely, then abandon it — "such is the price of greed." A "pseudo-stable" Age carries some instability but not enough for world-destroying effects.
- **Later-version rebalancing**: patch notes show Dense Ores instability increased, Accelerated made to add instability, and Villages/Strongholds/Mineshafts/Crystals/Tendrils/Dungeons set to add instability only *above certain stacking thresholds* — i.e., the designers kept tuning where "greed" begins. (The specific "~3 features before penalty" figure is community-reported and version-dependent; treat as approximate.) Because worst effects sit in the top 2 severity categories, a randomly-authored Age *rarely* shows decay/meteors; you mostly see mild debuffs unless you deliberately push value.

### Travel infrastructure
Linking Books capture a fixed return point; **crystal portals** (any shape, framed in Mystcraft crystal, with an 8-crystal book receptacle) turn a book into a permanent two-way gateway that entities can walk through. All linking is one-way per book, so you always carry a return book — and it's possible to get stranded in a dying Age.

### What fans loved / criticized
- **Loved**: the authorship-as-progression fantasy (start with a handful of symbols → crude worlds → hunt rarer symbols → compose precise, valuable worlds), the genuine risk/reward of greed, the Myst theming, and emergent "stable age" community sharing.
- **Criticized**: opaque grammar (players couldn't tell *why* an Age was unstable without the `/myst-dbg` console command that reads the hidden instability total); the no-erase, one-shot book commitment; fiddly page/notebook management; and reliance on wiki-diving. The bug where you "must" end books with Clear Modifiers to avoid instability is emblematic of the opacity.

### Successors / alternatives and their world-creation costs
- **RFTools Dimensions** (by McJty) — **the cleanest "anchoring cost" template.** You assemble **dimlets** (biome, terrain, block, structure, special) into a dimension tab; a **Dimension Builder** block spends **RF power** to *create* and then *maintain* the dimension. Every dimlet carries three numbers: **creation cost (RF/tick), tick cost (build time), and maintenance cost (RF/tick).** Baseline: any dimension needs ≥10 RF/tick to maintain; the cheapest void dimension costs ~100,000 RF to create + 10 RF/tick upkeep. A dimension has an internal buffer (40M RF); if the builder stops being powered, the dimension runs on the buffer — "a cheap dimension will last pretty long but an expensive dimension might only run for a minute." If the buffer drops below 10%, players inside are penalized (config: either kicked out or killed). A very expensive dimension observed needing 620k RF/tick. **Special Efficiency dimlets** reduce upkeep. This is *exactly* your "cost scales with world value/complexity, and worlds decay if you can't sustain them" idea, expressed as a clean resource-per-tick meter instead of Mystcraft's chaotic instability.
- **Dimensional Doors** (originally Stevenrs11) — pocket dimensions behind special doors. Highly relevant decay detail: in **Limbo** (the fail-state dimension you fall into by dying in a pocket, being seen by a Monolith, or overusing unstable doors), **"all blocks without an inventory, when placed, will begin a decay chain and eventually become Unraveled Fabric"** — a spreading entropy that punishes trying to build permanently in the wrong layer. Doors have a cost/rarity gradient (oak = free/common; gold = very expensive → treasure dungeon; unstable = eye-of-ender, randomly leads to a rift *or* Limbo). Rifts form when a door is destroyed ("no longer binding together the fabric of reality"). Personal Pockets are per-player private safe rooms — a "home base" layer analog.
- **Compact Machines / others**: pocket rooms as contained instanced spaces (couldn't research deeply this pass — flagged as a gap).

**Design levers for your game:**
1. **Split Mystcraft's chaos from RFTools' clarity.** Mystcraft's instability is thematically gorgeous but notoriously *opaque* (needed a debug command to read). Adopt RFTools' **legible upkeep meter** ("this world costs X sustain/tick, you produce Y") as the *primary* signal. Legibility is not softness: in-session crumbling can be as fast and dramatic as the diamond-sea fantasy demands — the fix for Mystcraft's flaw is that the player can *see why* it's happening and could *predict it before authoring*, not that it happens gently.
2. **Profiling-based cost is a self-balancing anchor.** Mystcraft's "measure how rare the contained blocks are, then charge instability" means the cost auto-scales to whatever the player actually built/harvested — you don't hand-tune every symbol. This directly serves "cost based on resource value / life contained / complexity."
3. **"Accept a lesser curse to buy stability"** (Charged-adds-stability) is a rich decision: let players opt into a manageable flaw (e.g., a world that's foggy, or slow-growing) to lower its anchoring cost — a trade the player authors rather than a flat penalty.

<summary>2. MYST lineage — theming vocabulary</summary>

The original Myst/Riven fiction gives you a ready-made, evocative vocabulary. The **D'ni** ("dunny") were an ancient civilization who practiced **"the Art"**: writing **Descriptive Books** whose text *links* to a pre-existing Age (worlds are found/described, not created — a lore point Gehn fatally misunderstands, believing he *creates* Ages). A **Linking Book** contains a **linking panel** (the "gateway image") you touch to travel; crucially, **a Linking Book must be written *inside* the Age and at the very spot it links to**, and you can write many linking books to one Age. **Trap Books / Prison Books** strand a reader. The Art's central discipline was **avoiding contradictions that cause instability** — precisely the mechanic Mystcraft gamified. Characters: **Atrus** (writer-protagonist), **Catherine**, **Gehn** (hubristic father), **Yeesha**. Vocabulary you can borrow: Age, the Art, Writer/Archivist, Descriptive vs. Linking Book, linking panel/gateway image, D'ni, instability, Star Fissure.

**Design lever:** The "worlds are *described/discovered*, not created" framing elegantly justifies both procedural generation *and* the idea that a poorly-written description yields an unstable/incoherent world — theme and mechanic reinforce each other.

<summary>3. FF12 GAMBIT SYSTEM + programmable automation as progression</summary>

### How gambits work
Each party member runs an ordered list of **gambit rules**, each = **(Condition/Target → Action)**. Execution: scan top-to-bottom every tick, **fire the first rule whose condition is true, then restart from the top.** Priority is strictly positional. Example: with "Foe: nearest → Attack" above "Ally HP<50% → Cure," healing never fires while an enemy exists; swap the order and healing takes priority. Higher-priority rules can *interrupt/cancel* a lower one mid-action (e.g., Steal on a full-HP foe before Attacking). Manual inputs always override gambits. Conditions include ally/foe HP/MP thresholds, status effects, elemental weaknesses, "party leader's target," nearest/highest-attack targeting, etc. Chains emerge: one character's buff can satisfy another's condition.

### How gambit pieces are acquired (this is the key "progression" detail)
- **Gambit condition/target pieces are bought at gambit shops or found in chests/urns** — you literally purchase "Ally: HP < 40%" as an item, and once obtained the *whole party* can use it. You hold only one copy of each.
- **Actions** unlock as you learn the underlying magick/technick/item.
- **Gambit slots** (how many rules a character can run) are **unlocked individually on each character's License Board.** Per the Final Fantasy Wiki: characters "start with no list of gambits, gain two automatically through the early game, and can buy licenses for an additional ten gambit slots in the Augments section of the License Board(s)" — i.e., a **0 → 2 → 12** progression, each slot spending License Points.
- **Zodiac Age (2017 remaster)** changes: added **job/class system** (each character picks Licenses from two of twelve jobs, so gambit *strategy* is now shaped by class role), plus quality-of-life (2×/4× speed, second license board). This tightened the identity of each automated role.

### What was loved / criticized
- **Loved**: "programming your party" and watching your logic execute a boss fight hands-off — "sit back, drink a cup of coffee and see the action unfold." Widely described as *programming for beginners* (if/else, priority). The set-and-forget elegance reduces tedium of trash fights while leaving room to intervene.
- **The "the game plays itself" debate**: critics argued gambits let the game auto-win; defenders (and the FFXII Sector guide author) counter that "the characters were doing the same thing I would have commanded them to do" — automation expresses *your* intent, freeing attention for the big picture (where to go next). Real criticism: setup opacity (ordering pitfalls), and that a fully-tuned party removes moment-to-moment engagement in easy content.

### Comparable programmable-automation systems
- **Dragon Age: Origins Tactics** — the closest cousin. Same condition→action rows. **Slots are earned progression**: 2 at level 1, +1 at levels 3/6/10/15/20/25/30, plus more by investing in the **Combat Tactics** skill. Notable comfort-vs-control choice: **the currently-selected character's tactics are disabled**, so you always hand-drive at least one member — a deliberate "don't fully automate the driver" rule.
- **Gladiabots** (also on iOS) — visual **if/then priority node trees** ("upside-down tree," first valid branch wins). Pure set-up-then-watch; you cannot intervene once a battle starts. Praised as "programming without having to remember the syntax"; "15 minutes of tutorial… years to master." Criticism: a battle you mis-programmed is a helpless watch, and grind/time-gating in the mobile version.
- **Unicorn Overlord** (Vanillaware, 2024) — **the best modern shipped proof that condition→action logic can be the spine of deep, long progression.** Explicitly compared to gambits. Each unit runs a **priority list** of skills, each with up to two conditions (e.g. "use Lean Edge IF user HP<75% AND AP≤2"). New skills/conditions arrive by recruiting and leveling **over 60 classes** (base + advanced, spread across the five nations of Fevrith, per Dot Esports). Designer Max Nichols called it "some of the best system design I've ever seen… a masterstroke," arguing the *act of authoring and testing tactics is itself the fun* — "a puzzle, a minigame." Criticism: condition evaluation order is unintuitive (players duplicate skills as a workaround).
- **Zachtronics (SHENZHEN I/O)** — a crucial **cautionary tale**: it originally **unlocked programming instructions/parts as you progressed**, then the developer *removed* most of that gating in a firmware update: "parts… now unlock automatically at the beginning… but 'soft-locked' to discourage inexperienced players." Reasons: hard-gating broke leaderboard fairness (you were compared to people with more parts) and frustrated optimizers. Residual design = **"recommended vs. not-recommended"** rather than locked.
- **while True: learn()** — visual ML-node puzzles unlock new nodes along a **timeline of real ML history** and a branching progress tree. **Cautionary criticism directly relevant to you**: hardware/node upgrades you buy later let you **"break" earlier puzzles** ("I found I was able to get gold using the exact same setup as before because the nodes processed faster… why let players break the puzzles in your puzzle game?"). Automation power outran the content.
- **Autonauts** — you program bots by **"record then replay"** block-visual scripts (move/pick/drop, loops, conditionals). Complexity is gated by **bot memory size, upgraded via research** (Mk0–Mk3, more memory = more complex programs); progress currency is "Wuv" from happy colonists. The whole game *is* the "it plays itself" flip: "you begin to see how automating processes will allow you to progress by freeing up your time." Tension noted by players: if late-game bots stay single-action, progression feels hollow.
- **Desynced** — node-based **Behavior Controllers** (a component you must equip to a unit to program it); automation is optional to progress. Criticized for a mid-game "nosedive" where "the game begins to resemble an idler… except you can't play another game while stuff happens."

**Design levers:**
1. **Gate the *pieces*, not just the slots.** FF12's "buy the condition 'Ally HP<40%' as an item" is a beautiful fit for your "logic pieces unlocked/purchased progressively" pillar — each new condition/action is a discrete, collectible upgrade with obvious value, and pieces are shared party-wide so acquisition feels generous.
2. **Keep one hand on the wheel (DA:O rule) or a manual-override (FF12 rule).** For an interruptible game, never let automation become a black box the player can only watch (the Gladiabots trap). Let the player always intervene, and consider disabling automation on the actively-selected unit.
3. **Scale content difficulty with automation power, or you trivialize your own game.** SHENZHEN and while True: learn() both show automation outrunning content. In your game this is *less* dangerous (trivializing trash is a *feature* — it reduces tedium), but design the "prestige" or hard content to assume full automation, so authoring stays meaningful.
4. **Automation as the reward for mastery, not a requirement.** DA:O/Desynced make automation optional; the joy is opting into it as you understand the system. This keeps early game hands-on, late game elegant.

<summary>4. WORLD DECAY / ENTROPY / UPKEEP — legibility, offline-safety, and tension allocation</summary>

Concrete upkeep models across games:
- **RFTools dimensions** (above): power/tick upkeep; buffer drains when unpowered; cheap worlds last long, expensive worlds die fast. Clean, legible, value-scaled.
- **Rust** — the canonical "maintain-or-lose" tax. A **Tool Cupboard** holds resources; every 24h it drains **~10% of the base's total build cost** in materials, and the rate **rises with size**: per Corrosion Hour's RUST Upkeep Building Guide, "after you've added more than 16 building structures to your base, the base's 10% upkeep tax will begin to rise… you can end up being upkeep taxed 30% of the total cost of your base every 24 hours" (progressive scaling so mega-bases pay disproportionately). When empty, structures **decay outermost-first** at tier-based rates (twig 1h, wood 3h, stone 5h, sheet metal 8h, armored 12h). The cupboard shows exact per-day cost. Design intent was anti-lag (kill abandoned mega-bases). The *legibility* (a meter that says "you're protected for N hours") is the part worth stealing; the real-time-while-offline drain is the part that conflicts with your interruptibility requirement.
- **Mystcraft instability** (above): entropy scaled to contained value; opaque; can be world-ending.
- **Dimensional Doors Limbo**: place blocks in the wrong layer → they decay to Unraveled Fabric. Entropy as a "you can't permanently settle here" signal.
- **Against the Storm** (see §5): the Blightstorm wipes worlds on a timer, framed as *seasonal renewal* rather than loss — and the countdown bar is deliberately imprecise "to achieve a sense of anxiety" (their words). That imprecision is a tunable anxiety knob, turnable in either direction.

### Tension allocation (replaces the earlier "cozy design tension" framing)
Cozy-genre literature argues for consistent low-stakes reward cycles ("no risk of failure resetting the cycle") on parasympathetic-response grounds. That describes one genre's contract, **not a requirement for this game** — your reference points (SPD, Slice & Dice, Mystcraft greed) are tense games that are comfy because of *pacing and turn structure*, not softness. The transferable kernels are only:
- **Legibility**: upkeep/decay must be readable and predictable *before commitment* (see §1 lever 1). Opaque loss generates resentment in any genre; legible loss generates stories.
- **Offline-safety**: nothing is silently taken from you while the app is closed or you've fallen asleep mid-turn. This is an interruptibility requirement, not a tension cap — in-session stakes can be as brutal as you like.
- **Loss framing is layer-dependent, and that's a design decision, not a default.** Your stated fantasy already embraces catastrophic loss (worlds disintegrating mid-harvest while you grab what you can). The open question is which layers permit permanent loss — not whether it's allowed. Losing a *disposable* world you strip-mined is the intended payoff of greed; the sanctuary question (is there a guaranteed-safe layer?) is Q10, unanswered.

**Design levers:**
1. **Two explicit world classes** (you already want this): *disposable* worlds have zero/low upkeep and are meant to be strip-mined and abandoned (Mystcraft Dense-Ores meta); *anchored* worlds cost ongoing sustain scaled to value. Make the *choice* to anchor deliberate and reversible.
2. **A single legible "sustain vs. produce" meter** per anchored world, RFTools-style, is the most legible possible upkeep UI — no hidden instability scores.
3. **Decay must be pause-safe.** For an interruptible iPhone game, upkeep must either freeze when you're not playing or tick only on in-session turns. This constrains *when* decay runs, not *how hard* it hits during play.

<summary>5. THREE-LAYER META-PROGRESSION precedents</summary>

Games that nest 3+ persistence layers (directly maps to your reality / home-base / instanced-run structure):

- **Against the Storm** — *the closest structural cousin.* Three layers: (1) **individual settlement runs** — "Each run lasts anywhere from 30 minutes to three hours… There is no real-time pressure — you can pause and save at any time" (Switchblade Gaming, 2026 guide), fully randomized blueprints/glade events/cornerstones; (2) the **World Map** of hex tiles you settle, farther = harder; (3) the **Smoldering City / Citadel** meta you permanently upgrade with meta-currency, "leaving a permanent mark on the World Map." The **Blightstorm cycle** periodically **wipes the entire World Map (regenerating biomes/modifiers) except the Citadel and its upgrades** — per the official wiki, "Every few decades, a cataclysm known as the Blightstorm occurs, and wipes the entire World Map clean… all settlements are destroyed, biomes are regenerated, and modifiers appear in new places." A cycle lasts a fixed number of in-game years (reforging Seals extends it — e.g. the Bronze Seal raises cycle length from 32 to 40 years), and the meta reset lands roughly every four or five runs. Runs feed meta-currency (earned win or lose; harder = more). Resource Points **must be spent before the storm or they reset** — a deliberate anti-hoarding rule ("stashing… ultimately leads to hours of boredom"). Widely praised for being pausable and free of fail-spirals; Steam-Deck friendly. **This is your three-layer + cyclical-reset thesis, already shipped and validated.**
- **Hades** — three interlocking persistence layers: (1) the **run**; (2) two persistent house systems — the **Mirror of Night** (spend Darkness on permanent talents; unlock deeper tiers with Chthonic Keys; refund/respec for 1 key) and the **House Contractor** (spend Gemstones/Diamonds on permanent renovations, *some of which alter future runs* by unlocking new chamber types — i.e., mid-arc persistent unlocks that change generation); (3) **Pact of Punishment / Heat** as a scalable post-clear difficulty/NG+ layer. Also multiple parallel meta-currencies (Darkness, Keys, Gems, Nectar, Titan Blood) each feeding a different system. Lesson: **layered currencies keep every run rewarding something**, but late-game currency overflow (maxed players with 15k useless gems) shows the risk of unbalanced sinks.
- **Rogue Legacy (1 & 2)** — (1) heir run; (2) the **Castle/Manor** of permanent gold-bought upgrades (stats, new classes, new dock shops, QoL like heir re-rolls); (3) **NG+** cycles + **Charon's Tribute / Soul Shop** that raise the upgrade *caps* themselves. Key detail: **starting NG+ hands all your gold to Charon** — a hard layer-transition sink. Manor upgrades hit **inflation ("Labor Costs") past level 30** so you can't max everything cheaply.
- **Prestige/incremental theory** — the deepest source on *why nested resets feel good*. Core loop: **reset a lower layer in exchange for a persistent currency that buys permanent multipliers**, then each higher layer resets everything below it. Community-documented failure modes you must avoid: (a) **reset must grant spendable currency + a clear preview of the gain** ("show 'After prestige your X earns 2.3×' so the reset feels like a reward not a loss") — resets without a visible payoff feel like pure loss; (b) avoid **"anti-upgrades"** that only speed up the next reset without helping now; (c) **gate complexity behind milestones** to avoid overwhelming players; (d) don't let currency-gain outstrip spendable sinks (the "stuck in a reset loop" complaint). Best practice: a **confirmation + preview** before any reset (players "accidentally prestiged" without one).

**Design levers:**
1. **Against the Storm is your reference implementation** for reality-layer-survives-a-cyclical-world-reset. Steal: cyclical renewal framed positively, meta-currency earned win-or-lose, and the *deliberate anti-hoarding* rule. Whether spend-or-lose pressure fits your game is your call — it's a knob, not a requirement (note it cuts against your hoarding-as-joy pillar, so if used, scope it to a specific currency, never to items).
2. **Hades' House Contractor is your model for "reality-layer unlocks that change mid-run generation."** Some permanent purchases should unlock *new world-symbols / chamber types* that then appear in future generated worlds — satisfying your "a bit of everything: NG+ persistent AND accessible mid-run" pillar.
3. **Every reset needs a visible, previewed payoff.** The #1 incremental-design lesson: never make a persistence-layer transition feel like loss; always show the concrete permanent gain first, and confirm before wiping.

<summary>6. PROCEDURAL WORLD-GEN FROM PLAYER-CHOSEN PARAMETERS (player as author)</summary>

The design space of "compose your world/run from collected parameters":

- **Dwarf Fortress world-gen** — the deepest *parametric* authorship. Players set: **mineral scarcity** (controls ore/gem frequency *and* which metals civs can access), **savagery** (0–100; too high makes maps uninhabitable), min/max + weighted ranges for **elevation, rainfall, temperature, drainage, volcanism**, mesh sizes/variance (how wildly adjacent tiles differ), counts of **megabeasts/semi-megabeasts/titans/demons/night creatures**, and **history length** (years simulated before play). Aggressive parameters risk **world rejection** (impossible combinations restart generation) or early civilization extinction. The player is a *meta-author setting constraints*; the sim authors history within them.
- **Minecraft custom/superflat/buffet** — direct, granular authorship: **Superflat** lets you define each block layer + biome via a **Customize menu or shareable preset codes** (9 built-in presets like Redstone Ready, Water World, The Void); **Buffet/Single-Biome** picks one biome to fill the Overworld; data-pack JSON presets let you author generation fully. Caveat: on Bedrock, flat worlds **disable achievements** — a "you opted out of the normal game" signal.
- **WorldBox** — god-sandbox blank canvas; players paint terrain/creatures and set **world size, world shape** (Pangea/islands/flat/round/volcanic), **noise level, random-shape amount, cubicle sizes**, then unleash emergent civilization + disasters. Joy = emergent unpredictability; criticism = repetitive without structured goals.
- **Townscaper** — **constrained authorship** via Wave Function Collapse. You only choose **where to place a block and its color**; the algorithm instantly resolves walls/roofs/stairs/gardens, and **color choices drive specific architecture** (multi-color enclosure → garden; stacked colors → lighthouse). Deliberately **non-deterministic** — "even if you click exactly the same way, the resulting town will not be exactly the same," which creator Oskar Stålberg "like[s] very much." A creative toy with no goals.
- **Islanders** — constrained authorship via **scoring**: place buildings for points based on adjacency (lumberjack near trees, away from other lumberjacks); hit a score threshold → unlock next building pack; place enough → unlock the next island. Overwhelmingly Positive; credited as a cozy-genre shaper. Authorship expressed through *optimization*, not free placement.
- **Roguelite "compose your run"** — **Hades Pact of Punishment** (14 modifiers, each with ranks/Heat values 0→64; more Heat = better rewards; "compose your difficulty"); **Slay the Spire Custom Mode** (toggle any modifiers + seed, but disables achievements/leaderboards); **Risk of Rain artifacts**; SPD challenges. These let you *spend/equip collected modifiers to shape the next run's rules* — the run-modifier analog of your symbol-authoring.
- **Loop Hero** (noted, not re-researched): its **deck-as-worldgen** — you place collected tiles/cards to build the loop's terrain and enemy composition — remains the closest *shipped* analog to "equip collected pieces to author the next world's generation," bridging deckbuilding and procedural authorship.
- **Mixed-initiative PCG (academic)** — the research term for "human + algorithm co-author content." Key design pillars (Lai et al., 2020): **"respect designer control, respect the creative process, respect existing work processes"** — a short feedback loop is essential so authoring doesn't disrupt flow; their study found mixed-initiative tools "increased engagement… effective in inspiring new ideas." Guzdial et al. define it: "a human designer works with an algorithm to produce game content… remains relatively under-explored." Design writers repeatedly frame the core tension as **"controlled variation that still feels intentional"** and note the authorship question: as the machine takes a bigger role, "designers are challenged to steer this generation toward outcomes that fulfill their creative vision."

**Design levers:**
1. **Constrained authorship (Townscaper/Islanders) fits a phone and a symbol economy better than parametric authorship (DF).** Not a coziness argument: DF's dozens of sliders don't fit a phone screen or one-handed play, and continuous sliders don't map onto discrete collectible symbols. "You choose the symbols; the grammar resolves the world beautifully" preserves authorship while keeping each choice a collectible, tradeable, meaningful object — which is your itemization pillar doing double duty.
2. **Non-determinism is a feature.** Stålberg's "same input, slightly different town" keeps authored worlds surprising — preserving your *exploration* joy even though the player *authored*. Fully-deterministic authoring would collapse into pure construction.
3. **Progressive symbol/parameter unlocks = built-in difficulty & content curve.** DF's "aggressive params → rejection/extinction" and Islanders' "score → unlock next pack" both show generation parameters doubling as a progression gate. Your symbol-collection loop already does this; lean into rare symbols unlocking *categories* of world, not just tweaks.

</details>

<details>
<summary>7. TURN-BASED iPHONE implementation references (brief)</summary>

Mechanics relevant only to session length, one-handed play, automation, nested persistence:
- **The Battle of Polytopia** — full 4X arc compressed into ~30-min matches (or 30-turn "Perfection" scoring mode); **touch-first UI from day one**, offline vs. AI, and **async multiplayer with a 24-hour turn timer** (the gold standard for interruptible turn-based on iOS). Passed **25 million organic mobile downloads as of Oct 28, 2025** (per Midjiwan/Games Press; up from 20M in Sept 2023). Demonstrates dual structure (empire macro + tile micro) on a phone.
- **Hoplite** — hex-grid turn-based roguelike; **attacks come from movement** (every step is the decision), runs last minutes, **pause and resume anytime**. Proof that deep tactics fit five-minute one-handed bursts.
- **Into the Breach** — bite-sized, fully deterministic tactical puzzles; flawless touch controls; perfect interruptibility (perfect-information turns).
- **Grindstone / Card Crawl / Card Thief** — puzzle-combat designed for one-handed, one-thumb sessions with persistent unlocks between runs.
- **General pattern**: the best iOS turn-based games are **perfect-information, pause-anywhere, offline-capable, and touch-native**, with any real-time/multiplayer element made **async with generous timers**. These are baseline requirements for your interruptible, sleep-friendly goal.

**Design lever:** Async-with-generous-timer (Polytopia) + pause-anywhere-perfect-information (Hoplite/Into the Breach) is the proven iOS template; your three-layer persistence should snapshot cleanly at *every* turn so the app can be killed at any moment with zero loss.

</details>

## Sharpest open design questions this research surfaces for Aimee

1. **What is the "sustain resource," and where does it come from?** Mystcraft charges *instability* (abstract), RFTools charges *RF power* (produced by machines in *other* dimensions). Does your anchoring cost draw from the home base, from *other* worlds (creating a portfolio/logistics layer), or from the anchored world itself (self-sustaining if rich enough)? This single choice defines your entire mid-game economy.
2. **Which layers permit permanent loss — and does offline time count?** Disposable-world death mid-harvest is your stated fantasy; the design decisions are (a) whether anchored worlds and the base can *ever* be permanently lost, (b) whether decay ticks only in-session or also while the app is closed, and (c) what the maximum loss from falling asleep mid-turn is (per §4, the working assumption is zero).
3. **Does upkeep tick in real time (while the app is closed) or only in-session?** This is existential for an interruptible iPhone game. If real-time, how do you prevent "punished for having a life"? If in-session only, does anchoring lose its tension?
4. **What exactly persists across the top-layer (reality) reset — and what's the *previewed reward* for triggering it?** Incremental theory says the reset must show its payoff first. What is the dopamine of a reality-layer reset, and what carries through (symbols? blueprints? multipliers? cosmetic marks on the world map à la Against the Storm)?
5. **Are the Gambit "logic pieces" collected the same way as world "symbols," or a parallel track?** You have two collection economies (world-authoring symbols + automation-logic pieces). Do they share a currency/acquisition loop (elegant, but risks competition) or run in parallel (richer, but more to explain)?
6. **How do you prevent automation from trivializing the authoring+harvesting joy?** SHENZHEN and while True: learn() both had automation outrun content. Since harvesting is core, does automating harvest *remove* the fun, or free you to author more/harder worlds? Should some high-value worlds resist automation (require hands-on play)?
7. **Constrained (Townscaper) or parametric (Dwarf Fortress) authorship?** How many "slots" does a world description have, and how much does the grammar resolve for the player vs. expose as dials? Fewer, more meaningful, collectible choices fit the phone and the symbol economy; more dials fit deep optimizers. Where on that axis do *you* want to sit?
8. **What's the fail-state grammar?** Mystcraft fills incomplete descriptions randomly and punishes contradictions. In *your* game, is an under-specified world a *happy surprise*, an *unstable danger*, or both depending on world type? Can a "bad" description ever brick a world, or only make it quirkier?
9. **How legible is the cost before you commit?** Mystcraft's opacity (needing a debug command) was its most-criticized flaw. Can the player *see* a world's projected anchoring cost / instability *before* authoring it, so composition is informed rather than gambling?
10. **Does interruptibility require one guaranteed-safe layer — and is the home base it?** Three layers invite three upkeep systems. If nothing may be silently lost while asleep, at least one layer probably needs to be a sanctuary where that's structurally guaranteed. Is that the base, the reality layer, or both — or is offline-freezing everywhere sufficient without any sanctuary?
